The Story

I built WorkGrid because I was
done switching tools.

This isn't a polished corporate origin story. It's the honest, messy, frustrating journey that led to WorkGrid — told by the person who built it.

Solo Developer & Founder

Building WorkGrid in public, one commit at a time

Chapter 01

The problem nobody was solving

It started the way most bad situations start: slowly.

I was managing a small team — 5 people, a handful of clients, a growing list of tasks. Nothing complicated. The kind of operation that should be manageable with a whiteboard and some discipline.

But whiteboards don't work remotely. So I opened my laptop and did what everyone does: I went looking for a tool.

I didn't need a CRM. I didn't need a project manager. I needed a place where my team could work — and I couldn't find one.

Every tool I tried was either too much or too little. HubSpot wanted me to build a marketing empire when I just wanted to track three deals. Monday.com gave me beautiful boards but collapsed the moment I needed invoicing. ClickUp had so many features that my team stared at the screen like deer in headlights.

And then there was Notion. Oh, Notion. The tool everyone recommends when they don't want to give you a real answer.

Chapter 02

The tool-hopping timeline

Two years. Six tools. Zero satisfaction. Here's what actually happened.

Early 2022

Started with Trello

Free, simple, got us started. But within a month we hit the wall: no CRM features, no invoicing, no way to track deals. Trello is a great kanban board and nothing else.

Mid 2022

Switched to Notion (the "build anything" phase)

Someone told me "you can build a CRM in Notion!" So I did. Spent two weeks setting up databases, relations, formulas, automations. It worked — until I needed to send an invoice and realized I'd have to build that too. Then HR. Then attendance. At some point you're not using a tool — you're building one.

Late 2022

Tried HubSpot (the "proper CRM" phase)

Signed up for the free tier. Immediately hit the "upgrade to see this" wall. Upgraded to Starter at $20/user/mo. Realized we still couldn't do invoicing. Still couldn't do HR. Still needed Trello for tasks because HubSpot's task management is an afterthought. Now paying for two tools and using both poorly.

Early 2023

Gave Monday.com a shot

Beautiful boards. Genuinely nice UI. But "CRM template" is just a board with a "Deal Stage" column — no pipeline logic, no forecasting, no contact profiles. And still no invoicing, no HR. The marketing said "work OS" but it was really "task OS."

Mid 2023

Briefly tried ClickUp

Installed it. Opened it. Saw approximately 47 menu items, 12 view types, and a settings page that looked like the cockpit of a 747. My team's reaction: "absolutely not." Uninstalled within a week. No invoicing. No HR. More features than any human needs but missing the two things we actually needed.

Late 2023

The breaking point

I sat down and counted: we were using Notion for docs, Stripe for invoicing, a shared Google Sheet for HR/attendance, and email for deal tracking. Four tools. None of them talking to each other. I was spending 3+ hours a week just moving data between them. That's when I stopped looking and started building.

Chapter 03

The moment I snapped

It was a Thursday evening. I was copying invoice data from Stripe into a Google Sheet so our "HR person" (who was actually just the most organized person on the team) could see what clients had paid. Then I had to update the deal status in HubSpot. Then I had to mark the related tasks as done in Notion.

Same data. Three places. Manual entry. Every single time.

I looked at my screen — four tabs open, all showing the same information in different formats — and thought: "This is insane. I'm a developer. Why am I doing this manually?"

That night I opened my code editor. Didn't plan anything. Didn't make a Notion database for planning (ironic, I know). Just started building a workspace where tasks, deals, invoices, and HR lived in one place.

It was ugly. It was broken. It was mine.

Chapter 04

What WorkGrid is actually meant to be

WorkGrid isn't trying to be the next Salesforce. It's not trying to be the next HubSpot. It's trying to be the tool I couldn't find two years ago.

Here's the core idea, stated as simply as I can:

A small team should be able to manage tasks, track deals, send invoices, and run HR from one workspace — without paying enterprise prices or hiring a consultant to set it up.

That's it. That's the whole vision. I'm not trying to disrupt a $100B industry or build a unicorn startup. I'm trying to solve a problem that I and thousands of other small teams face every day: tool fragmentation.

The average small team uses 5-8 SaaS tools. Each one costs money. Each one has a learning curve. None of them talk to each other properly. The result is a patchwork of apps held together by copy-paste and hope.

WorkGrid is my answer to that. Not by being the best CRM, or the best project tool, or the best invoicing app — but by being good enough at all of them that you don't need anything else.

Chapter 05

Things I believe about software

Not mission statements. Not values printed on a wall. Actual beliefs that shape every decision I make building WorkGrid.

Simple beats powerful

A tool that 10 people actually use is better than a tool that 100 people could use but don't. Every feature I add gets weighed against: "Will this make someone close the tab?"

2 minutes, not 2 weeks

If your onboarding takes more than a coffee break, you've failed. WorkGrid should feel obvious the first time you open it. No tutorials required.

Privacy isn't a feature — it's a right

HR data should never be visible to sales. Deal values should never be visible to interns. Role-based access isn't optional — it's the default.

Good enough at everything > perfect at one thing

WorkGrid's invoicing won't beat Stripe. Its CRM won't beat Salesforce. But having all four in one place beats juggling all of them separately. Every time.

Honesty over marketing

Check our comparison page. We list what WorkGrid can't do. We list competitors' strengths. If you need something we don't have, I'd rather tell you upfront than waste your time.

Ship fast, listen faster

I'd rather ship an imperfect feature this week and iterate than ship a perfect feature next quarter. The changelog is public. The roadmap is public. Progress is visible.

Chapter 06

Building in public

I made a deliberate choice to build WorkGrid transparently. Not because it's a growth hack (though it helps), but because I think software should be built by people who are accountable.

What "building in public" means here:

Public Changelog

Every feature, fix, and improvement documented with dates

Public Roadmap

See what's coming next — no surprise paywalls

Honest About Bugs

If something's broken, I say it's broken. No spinning.

Direct Access to Me

Not a support ticket black hole — actual conversations

WorkGrid is a solo project. That's not a weakness — it's a feature. There's no PM deciding what to build based on a spreadsheet. There's no sales team pushing for enterprise features while ignoring what small teams need. There's just me, my code editor, and the feedback from people who actually use it.

I'd rather have 100 users who love WorkGrid than 10,000 users who tolerate it.

Chapter 07

A bit about me

I'm a full-stack developer who got tired of building tools for other people while struggling with my own tool stack. That contradiction — building software for a living but being unable to find software that works for me — is what drove this project.

I work late at night, fueled by coffee and the irrational belief that one person can build something that competes with VC-backed companies. Maybe that's naive. Maybe that's exactly what makes it work.

When I'm not coding WorkGrid, I'm probably reading about product design, complaining about SaaS pricing, or playing video games to reset my brain.

Chapter 08

What I'm asking from you

I'm not asking you to pay. WorkGrid is free during early access — no credit card, no trial limits, no "upgrade to unlock" tricks.

What I am asking is this:

01

Try it

Actually use it for a week. Not just open it and close it. Create a workspace, add some tasks, make a deal, send a fake invoice. See if it clicks.

02

Tell me what's broken

I'm one person. I will miss things. When you find a bug, a confusing flow, or a missing feature — tell me. I read every message.

03

Share it with one person

If WorkGrid solves a problem for you, someone you know probably has the same problem. A single share means more than any ad I could run.

That's it. No growth hacks, no referral programs, no "invite 5 friends to unlock features." Just try it, be honest about it, and share it if you think it's good.
